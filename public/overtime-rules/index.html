Overtime Rules by State

Updated October 21, 2026 · Plain-English guide · How we write

Most US hourly workers fall under the federal weekly overtime rule, but a few states add daily thresholds or double time. This guide explains the rule we can document from official sources, shows how each one changes a paycheck, and links to the calculator that applies it.

The federal rule (FLSA)

The Fair Labor Standards Act requires covered employees who are not exempt to be paid at least one and one-half times their regular rate for hours worked over 40 in a workweek. The U.S. Department of Labor defines the workweek as a fixed and regularly recurring period of 168 hours: seven consecutive 24-hour periods. It need not be a calendar week and can start on any day or hour. Two points matter for every calculation:

  • No averaging. The Department of Labor states that averaging hours over two or more weeks is not permitted. A 50-hour week and a 30-hour week contain 10 hours of overtime, not zero.
  • No daily overtime by itself. Federal overtime is measured by the workweek. The Department notes that the Act does not require overtime pay for work on Saturdays, Sundays, holidays or rest days as such, and an agreement that only 8 hours a day or 40 hours a week counts as working time does not satisfy the Act.

The regular rate includes all remuneration for employment, with specific exclusions such as discretionary bonuses, expense reimbursements and pay for leave. A non-discretionary bonus therefore raises the rate that overtime is based on. Try it in the overtime calculator.

Where states add daily overtime

States can require more than the federal law. These four are implemented in our calculators, each with the agency source linked below.

Every other state has its own wage-hour law, and many simply follow the federal rule; some set different weekly thresholds or industry-specific rules. We do not list them because we can only publish what we can cite: look up your state labor department's overtime page, and use the “Federal” option in our calculators only if your state follows that standard.

Worked examples: same hours, different pay

Three days of 10 hours plus one of 12, at $25.00

Mon 10, Tue 10, Wed 10, Thu 12 = 42 hours.

  • Federal: 40 regular + 2 overtime = 40 × $25 + 2 × $37.50 = $1,000 + $75 = $1,075.00.
  • California: Mon-Wed: 2 overtime hours each (6); Thu: 4 overtime hours (hours 9-12) = 10 overtime hours, 32 regular. Pay = 32 × $25 + 10 × $37.50 = $800 + $375 = $1,175.00.
  • Colorado: no day passes 12 hours, so only the weekly rule applies: 2 overtime hours, $1,075.00.
  • Alaska: same as California without double time: 10 daily overtime hours, regular-by-day hours 32 (below 40 so no weekly overtime), $1,175.00.

The $100 gap between the federal and California results is the cost of the daily rule for this particular week.

California's seventh day and double time

According to the California Department of Industrial Relations, double pay applies to hours beyond 12 in a workday and to hours beyond 8 on the seventh consecutive day of work in a workweek. In the calculator the seventh-day rule applies only when all seven days of the workweek have hours. A week of 8 hours Monday to Sunday (56 hours) is 8 hours at the weekly 1.5x for hours over 40, plus 8 hours on the seventh day at 1.5x, for 16 overtime hours and 40 regular hours.

How to pick the right rule in a calculator

  1. Choose the rule for the state where you work, not where you live.
  2. If you are not sure whether you are exempt from overtime, read the exempt vs non-exempt guide first.
  3. Enter hours actually worked per day, starting with the first day of your employer's workweek.
  4. Compare the result with your pay stub. If it differs, ask payroll to explain; then see time card mistakes.

Frequently asked questions

When does overtime start?

Under federal law, after 40 hours in a workweek. California, Alaska, Colorado and (for lower-paid workers) Nevada also have daily thresholds, as described above.

Does every state have daily overtime?

No. Many states follow the federal weekly standard only. Check your state labor department for your own rules.

Do I get overtime if I am salaried?

Only if you are non-exempt. The salary basis is one of several tests for exemption; see the exempt vs non-exempt guide.

Can my employer give me time off instead of overtime pay?

Rules on compensatory time are narrow and depend on the employer and the state. For example, Nevada's regulations say an employer may not substitute compensatory time for overtime wages, and Colorado's COMPS Order does not allow comp time for covered private employers. Ask your state labor department.

Does a holiday count as overtime?

The Department of Labor says the FLSA does not require overtime pay for work on holidays as such. Your employer or union contract may pay a premium.
