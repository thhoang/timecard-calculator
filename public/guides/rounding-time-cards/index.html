Rounding Time Cards: What Is Allowed?

Updated October 22, 2026 · Plain-English guide · How we write

Time clocks often round your punches to the nearest 5, 6 or 15 minutes. Federal regulation permits the practice in some circumstances. This guide explains what the rule actually says and how to check whether rounding at your workplace is fair.

What the regulation says

29 CFR 785.48(b) addresses “rounding” practices. It recognises the practice, used for many years, of recording employees' starting and stopping time to the nearest 5 minutes, or to the nearest one-tenth or quarter of an hour. It treats this as acceptable for enforcement purposes where the practice is applied in a way that will not result, over a period of time, in failure to compensate the employees properly. In other words, rounding is allowed when it is neutral: sometimes up, sometimes down, and on average it does not cost workers time.

The regulation does not use the phrase “7-minute rule”. That is the common name for how quarter-hour rounding works in practice (below). The rule also does not say your state allows rounding: states may have their own limits, so check your state labor department.

How the 7-minute rule works

With a quarter-hour grid, the marks are :00, :15, :30 and :45. A punch within 7 minutes of a mark rounds to that mark; a punch 8 minutes or more past a mark rounds forward to the next one. Rounding to 5 minutes and 6 minutes (tenths of an hour) works the same way with a smaller grid. Use the time rounding calculator to test your own punches.

A fairness test with real numbers

Is this rounding neutral?

An employee arrives early to get ready and clocks in at 7:53 every morning and out at exactly 4:30. The 7:53 punch is 8 minutes past 7:45, so it rounds up to 8:00 and the employee loses 7 minutes of paid time a day. Over 20 days that is 140 minutes (2 hours 20 minutes) of unpaid work, worth $46.67 at $20.00/hour. If the same employee clocked in at 7:52 instead, the punch would round down to 7:45 and gain 7 minutes. Rounding is neutral only if both kinds of punches appear about equally. If most punches fall where rounding goes against the employee — for example when workers must clock in a few minutes early to get ready — the rounding shortchanges them every day.

What to do: keep a log of actual punches for a month, total the rounded and unrounded hours using the time card calculator, and see which way the difference runs. A persistent gap in one direction is the pattern the regulation's “over a period of time” language is aimed at.

Common questions employers and employees ask

  • Can the employer always round down? Rounding that systematically favors the employer is the practice that attracts scrutiny; the regulation's condition is that it does not result in failure to compensate employees properly over time.
  • Should rounding apply to start and end, or to the total? The regulation speaks of recording starting and stopping time; punch rounding is the standard application. Rounding a total can distort overtime differently.
  • What about rounding that crosses the 40-hour line? Overtime depends on the rounded hours your employer pays; a few minutes can mean a few overtime minutes. See the example in the overtime calculator.
  • Do states ban rounding? Some may; we only publish what we can cite, so check your state labor department's guidance.

Tips for employees and small employers

  • Employees: keep your own record and compare it to the pay stub every period.
  • Employers: if you round, document the rule, apply it to every punch in both directions and audit periodically with a sample of real punches.
  • If time to the minute is practical, many employers simply pay to the minute, which avoids the question.

Alternatives to rounding

  • Pay to the minute. Modern systems calculate exact minutes; it is simple to explain.
  • Grace periods with exact pay. Allow a short window for attendance policy without changing pay.
  • Rounding in tenths. Round to 6 minutes so each entry is a one-decimal number, if the policy is neutral.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 7-minute rule?

The common name for rounding punches to the nearest quarter hour: 1-7 minutes past a mark rounds down and 8-14 minutes rounds up.

Is rounding to the nearest 15 minutes legal?

Federal regulation 29 CFR 785.48(b) accepts rounding to the nearest 5 minutes, tenth or quarter hour when it does not, over time, result in failure to compensate employees properly. State law may add limits.

Can my employer round my time in their favor only?

A practice that consistently favors the employer risks failing the regulation's condition. If you think it applies to you, document your actual punches and ask payroll or your labor department.

Does rounding apply to breaks?

Check your employer's policy; the regulation discusses starting and stopping time.

How can I check my rounding?

Use the rounding calculator on your real punches, and compare rounded and actual totals over several weeks.
