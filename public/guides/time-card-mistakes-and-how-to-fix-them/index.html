Time Card Mistakes and How to Fix Them

Updated October 21, 2026 · Plain-English guide · How we write

Almost everyone has had a time card that was wrong. Most errors are small and easy to fix if you spot them in the same pay period and bring evidence.

The seven most common mistakes

  1. Missed punch. You forgot to clock in or out. The system estimates or leaves the day blank.
  2. Wrong AM/PM. 8:00 PM entered instead of 8:00 AM turns an 8-hour day into 12 hours or zero.
  3. Lunch not taken but deducted. An automatic 30-minute deduction on a day you worked through lunch.
  4. Decimal vs minutes. 7:30 typed as 7.30 pays 7 hours 18 minutes. See the decimal converter.
  5. Rounding that always goes one way. See rounding time cards.
  6. Overtime not applied. More than 40 hours in the workweek (or the state daily threshold) paid at straight time.
  7. Wrong workweek or pay period. Hours land in the wrong week, which shifts the overtime split.

How to fix a mistake: step by step

  1. Write down what really happened, with dates and times, as soon as you notice.
  2. Collect evidence. Messages, schedules, door-badge records, or a coworker who can confirm.
  3. Recalculate the day and the week using the time card calculator so you know the exact difference in hours and pay.
  4. Submit a correction request to your supervisor or payroll in writing (email is ideal) before the payroll cutoff.
  5. Check the next pay stub. The correction may appear as an adjustment in a later period.
  6. Escalate if needed. Ask payroll to explain in writing. If the issue repeats or you are told it will not be paid, contact your state labor department or the U.S. Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division.

Worked examples

Missed clock-out

You clocked in 8:00 am and forgot to clock out; you left at 4:45 pm after a 30-minute lunch. Correct hours = 8:45 minus 0:30 = 8:15 (8.25 hours). At $17.00 an hour that is $140.25 for the day. Send: “I forgot to clock out Tuesday 3/10; I left at 4:45 pm. Please correct to 8:15 paid hours.”

Overtime paid at straight time

Your timesheet shows 44 hours at $20.00, but the stub pays $880.00 (44 × $20). The correct gross under the federal weekly rule is 40 × $20 + 4 × $30 = $920.00. The difference is the missing overtime premium: 4 × $10 = $40.00. Overtime for a workweek is generally due on the regular payday for the pay period in which the workweek ends (29 CFR 778.106), so ask for the adjustment promptly.

Decimal entry error

A 7:45 shift typed as 7.45 hours pays 7 hours 27 minutes, 18 minutes short. Over five days that is 1.5 hours, or $27.00 at $18.00 an hour.

Prevention

  • Check your time card at the end of every shift or at least every Friday.
  • Take a screenshot or photo of the period before it is submitted.
  • Use one time format everywhere.
  • For recurring shifts, set a calendar reminder to punch out.
  • If you manage others: ask staff to review their cards before the cutoff and keep a log of corrections, with who approved each one.

A simple correction request you can copy

Subject: Time card correction - [date]
Hello [name], on [date] I clocked in at [time] and out at [time] with a [length] unpaid meal. The card shows [what it shows]. The correct paid time is [hours:minutes]. Please correct it before the [date] payroll cutoff. Thank you.

Keep a copy. If the correction is declined, ask for the reason in writing.

Special cases that cause errors

  • Overnight shifts assigned to the wrong day, which can move hours into the wrong workweek.
  • Transfers between departments or pay rates during one shift, where each block needs its own rate.
  • Holidays where the premium is applied to the wrong hours.
  • Daylight saving time changes, when a night shift is an hour longer or shorter than the clock shows.
  • Edits by a supervisor after you signed the card.

For managers: a correction log

Keep a record of every change: the date, the original entry, the corrected entry, who requested it, who approved it and why. A log makes payroll audits simple and protects both employee and employer. Corrections should be made the same pay period when possible.

How long a correction can take

Policies differ. Some employers correct errors in the next payroll; others require a form and manager approval. Overtime due for a workweek is generally due on the regular payday for the period in which the workweek ends, so a prompt request matters, and if the amount cannot be determined until after the pay period it should be paid as soon as practicable (29 CFR 778.106). Keep dates for every request you make.

If you are an employer, treat each correction as a record: who requested it, what changed, who approved it and when it was paid.

A weekly five-minute audit

Every Friday, check five things: every day has both punches, the AM/PM looks right, the meal deductions match what you took, the weekly total matches your own count and any hours over 40 are marked as overtime. Fix anything immediately. Five minutes a week prevents most disputes.

Frequently asked questions

What do I do if I forgot to clock out?

Tell your supervisor as soon as you can, give the actual time you left and ask for a correction. Keep a written record.

Can I fix a time card after it was submitted?

Usually you request a correction through your supervisor or payroll. Policies and cutoffs vary.

What if my employer won't fix an error?

Keep your records and contact your state labor department or the U.S. Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division.

How long should I keep my own time records?

Keep them at least until your pay is confirmed correct, and longer if a dispute is open. Employers have their own retention rules under federal and state law.

Is the error my fault if I signed the card?

A signature confirms what you reviewed, but it does not by itself determine what you were owed. Raise any error you notice.
