How this was calculated

    How semi-monthly periods work

    Semi-monthly pay periods follow the calendar, not the 7-day workweek. The first period is the 1st through the 15th. The second is the 16th through the last day of the month, which is 13 days in a non-leap February and up to 16 days in a 31-day month. That is why the number of weekdays in a period changes: March 2026 has 10 weekdays from the 1st to the 15th but 12 from the 16th to the 31st (the tool shows the exact dates).

    The workweek, however, is still seven days. When a workweek begins in one period and ends in the next, overtime for that week depends on all of its hours. If you are completing the second period, enter the hours you worked earlier in that same workweek in Hours already worked earlier this workweek, and the calculator counts them toward the 40-hour threshold.

    Worked example: a workweek that straddles the 15th

    January 2026, workweek starting Monday

    Monday January 12 to Thursday January 15 an employee works four 10-hour days (40 hours), all in the first period (1st-15th). On Friday January 16, the first day of the second period, they work 8 more hours.

    • The workweek (Jan 12-18) totals 48 hours, so under the federal weekly rule 8 hours are overtime.
    • When filling in the 16th-to-month-end card, type 40 in the box for hours already worked earlier this workweek. The calculator then shows the 8 hours on Friday as overtime.
    • At $24.00/hour those 8 hours earn 8 × $36.00 = $288.00 instead of $192.00 at straight time.

    Under 29 CFR 778.106, overtime pay is due on the regular payday for the pay period in which the workweek ends. This workweek ends Sunday January 18, in the second period, so that is the paycheck that should include the overtime for all 8 hours. Ask your payroll department how it handles the straddling week.

    Semi-monthly vs bi-weekly in one paragraph

    Bi-weekly checks arrive every other week (26 a year) and always cover exactly two workweeks. Semi-monthly checks arrive twice a month (24 a year) and each one covers roughly half a month. A salaried employee gets the same amount each time (annual salary ÷ 24), but an hourly employee's check varies with how many working days fall in the period. See the full comparison.

    A quick way to estimate a semi-monthly check

    For an hourly employee, count the working days in the period and multiply by the paid hours per day and the hourly rate. For example, March 1-15, 2026 contains 10 weekdays, so 10 × 8 hours = 80 hours; March 16-31 contains 12 weekdays, so 12 × 8 = 96 hours. At $22.00 an hour the two checks are $1,760 and $2,112 before taxes, even though the monthly total of 176 hours is simply 22 working days. Salaried employees do not see this swing because their check is annual salary ÷ 24.

    Entering part of a month

    If you started or ended a job mid-period, enter only the days you worked and leave the rest blank. The result is the total for the days you entered. For a final paycheck or a partial first paycheck, compare the total to your pay stub; some employers pay partial periods by days instead of hours, so ask how yours is calculated.

    Frequently asked questions

    How many days are in a semi-monthly pay period?

    The first period is always 15 days (1st to 15th). The second is 13 to 16 days depending on the month's length.

    How many pay periods are in a year if I am paid semi-monthly?

    24: two for each of the 12 months.

    What if a workweek spans two pay periods?

    Overtime is based on the whole workweek. Enter the hours already worked earlier in the week in the 'Hours already worked' box so the second period is calculated correctly.

    Do I get the same check every semi-monthly period?

    A salaried employee does. An hourly employee's check changes with the hours worked in each period.

    Is semi-monthly pay legal everywhere?

    Pay frequency is set by state law. The U.S. Department of Labor publishes a state-by-state payday table; for example it lists semi-monthly pay as an option in Nevada and Alaska. Check your state labor department for your own rules.
